Solar generators solve that problem entirely. Zero emissions, silent operation, and they run safely inside your house. Here's how to pick the right one for winter.

Cold Weather Matters: Self-Heating Batteries

This is where winter backup differs from summer backup. LiFePO4 batteries — the type used in all modern solar generators — cannot charge below 32°F (0°C) without a self-heating feature. They can discharge down to about -4°F (-20°C), but if you need to recharge from solar panels during a cold spell, you need a model with built-in battery heating.

Models with self-heating batteries:

  • EcoFlow DELTA Pro — self-heating down to -4°F
  • BLUETTI AC300 + B300 — self-heating battery modules
  • ALLPOWERS R3500 — self-heating LiFePO4
  • EcoFlow DELTA Pro Ultra — self-heating, rated to -4°F
Important: If your solar generator does NOT have self-heating, keep it indoors or in a heated garage during winter. Attempting to charge a cold LiFePO4 battery below freezing can permanently damage it. Discharging (using it) in cold weather is safe — it's only charging that's the problem.

Winter Storm Prep Checklist

Buy the solar generator before storm season. Prices spike and inventory sells out when storms hit the news. Here's how to get ready:

  1. Charge to 100% before every storm warning. The DELTA 2 Max does wall-to-full in 1.7 hours. DELTA Pro takes 2.7 hours. Keep it topped off from October through March.
  2. Test your setup. Plug in your fridge, furnace fan, and lights. Run for 2 hours and check the battery drain. Now you know your real runtime.
  3. Position solar panels. If you have panels, identify a south-facing window or a clear spot in the yard. In winter, low sun angles mean panels work best angled at 50–60°. Even cloudy winter days produce 20–30% of rated solar input.
  4. Stock electric blankets. An electric blanket (100W) keeps you warm for 20 hours on a 2,000Wh battery. A space heater (1,500W) drains the same battery in 1.3 hours.
  5. Keep the unit indoors. LiFePO4 batteries lose capacity in extreme cold. Store and operate your solar generator in a heated room, garage, or basement.
  6. Have a backup charging plan. Keep your car topped off — most solar generators can charge from a 12V car outlet. DELTA Pro does car-to-full in ~13 hours, enough to top off while the storm passes.

Solar Generator vs Gas Generator for Winter: The Safety Question

Every winter, the same story: an ice storm knocks out power, families bring gas generators inside or into their garages, and carbon monoxide does the rest. The CDC, CPSC, and every fire department in the country say the same thing: never run a gas generator indoors or in an enclosed space.

Solar generators eliminate that risk entirely:

  • Zero carbon monoxide — run indoors, in a bedroom, right next to your bed
  • Zero noise — won't wake the house or anger neighbors
  • Zero fuel — no gas cans, no fuel runs, no stale gas problems
  • Zero maintenance — charge it and forget it until you need it

The tradeoff: gas generators produce more sustained power for heavy loads like central HVAC. But for the 90% of winter outages that last 4–24 hours, a 2,000–3,600Wh solar generator covers everything most households need — safely.

FAQ

Can a solar generator power a space heater during a winter storm?

Technically yes, if the unit has 1,500W+ output. But it drains the battery fast — a 1,000W heater empties a 2,000Wh battery in about 2 hours. Better strategy: use an electric blanket (50–200W) which runs 10–20 hours on the same battery, or use a portable propane heater for room heat and save the battery for electronics.

Do solar generators work in cold weather?

Yes. LiFePO4 batteries can discharge (power your devices) in temperatures as low as -4°F (-20°C). The limitation is charging: most LiFePO4 batteries can't charge below 32°F. Models with self-heating batteries (DELTA Pro, AC300, R3500) handle this automatically. Keep your unit indoors when possible.

What size solar generator do I need for winter?

For lights, phones, Wi-Fi, and a CPAP: 500–1,000Wh ($219–$500). For those plus a fridge: 1,500–2,000Wh ($1,000–$1,300). For full essentials including a furnace blower: 3,000Wh+ ($1,400–$1,900). For multi-day outages: expandable system or 5,000Wh+ ($2,500+).

Should I get a solar generator or a gas generator for winter?

Solar generators are safer — zero carbon monoxide, run indoors. Gas generators produce more continuous power but must run outside (even in a storm). For most winter outages (4–24 hours), a 2,000–3,600Wh solar generator covers household essentials safely. Only consider gas if you need to run central HVAC or heavy equipment for extended periods.
