Hurricane Season Is Here. The Grid Will Fail. Are You Ready?

Hurricane season 2026 runs from June 1 through November 30, and NOAA is forecasting above-average activity for the Atlantic basin this year. That means more named storms, more landfalls, and more power outages — potentially lasting days or even weeks for hard-hit areas.

Here is the reality most people learn too late: the grid is the first thing to fail during a hurricane. High winds snap power lines. Flooding takes out substations. Fallen trees tear transformers off poles. In 2024, Hurricane Helene left over 6 million customers without electricity across the Southeast. Hurricane Milton knocked out power to 3.4 million in Florida just two weeks later. Some areas waited 10–14 days for restoration. In 2025, Hurricane Rafael caused multi-day blackouts across the Gulf Coast.

If you wait until a storm is announced to buy a generator, you are already too late. Units sell out within 48 hours of a hurricane watch being issued. Prices spike. Shipping delays push delivery past the storm window. The time to prepare is before hurricane season peaks — which means right now.

A solar generator for hurricane preparedness solves the three biggest problems with traditional gas generators: it runs safely indoors (zero emissions), it recharges from free sunlight after the storm passes, and it requires zero fuel during the exact situation when gas stations are closed or out of fuel. You charge it from a wall outlet before the storm, ride out the outage on battery power, then deploy solar panels to recharge once skies clear.

We evaluated the current crop of high-capacity power stations specifically for hurricane and storm scenarios — looking at battery capacity for overnight coverage, output wattage for running fridges and medical devices, solar recharge speed for multi-day outages, and overall durability. These are the five we would trust to keep our own families powered through a Category 3 landfall.

Hurricane Prep Checklist: What to Do Before the Storm

Owning a solar generator is step one. Having it ready to perform when you need it is what actually keeps your family safe. Here is the checklist we recommend the moment a hurricane watch or warning is issued for your area.

Timing matters: Do not wait until the storm is 24 hours out. Complete this checklist as soon as a watch is issued (typically 48 hours before expected conditions). Stores will be packed, gas stations will have lines, and online orders will not arrive in time.

Power Prep (48 Hours Out)

  • Charge your solar generator to 100% from a wall outlet. Most units charge fully in 1.5–3 hours. Do this first — if the grid goes down early, you want maximum capacity.
  • Charge all expansion batteries to 100% if you have them.
  • Charge all portable battery banks, laptops, tablets, and rechargeable flashlights. These become secondary power reserves.
  • Test your solar panels. Connect them to your generator in direct sunlight and confirm they are producing power. Check cables for damage. Fix any issues now, not during the storm.
  • Download the generator app (BLUETTI, EcoFlow, or ALLPOWERS) and confirm you can connect via Bluetooth. Familiarize yourself with the interface and monitoring features.
  • Identify your essential circuits. Know which outlets power your fridge, router, and medical equipment. Label them if they are not already labeled on your breaker panel.

Home Prep (48–24 Hours Out)

  • Freeze water bottles and fill freezer bags with ice. A full freezer stays cold longer during an outage (thermal mass). Frozen water bottles become drinking water as they melt.
  • Set your fridge and freezer to the coldest setting. Pre-cooling maximizes the time food stays safe without power.
  • Fill bathtubs with water for flushing toilets (if you are on a well pump that loses power).
  • Position your solar panels in an accessible but sheltered location. Do NOT deploy them outside during the storm. Store them indoors and plan where you will set them up once the storm passes.
  • Move the generator to its operating position — ideally a central ground-floor location away from windows, near the appliances you will power.
  • Run extension cords to your fridge, router, and key outlets. Pre-routing means you are not fumbling in the dark when the power drops.
  • Fill your car's gas tank. Your car can charge your solar generator via 12V if solar is unavailable for an extended period.

During the Storm

  • Monitor battery level via the app. Keep track of your watt-hours remaining and estimated runtime.
  • Prioritize loads. Fridge and medical devices first. Router second. Lights and device charging third. Window AC only if you have capacity to spare.
  • Turn off the fridge periodically if you need to extend runtime. A closed fridge stays cold for 4–6 hours, a full freezer for 24–48 hours. Cycling it on and off can stretch your battery life significantly.
  • NEVER run a gas generator indoors. This is why a solar generator is the safer choice — zero emissions, safe for indoor use.

After the Storm

  • Deploy solar panels as soon as conditions are safe. Position them in direct sunlight. Even partial sun produces meaningful charge.
  • If power flickers back on, immediately plug in the generator to recharge from the wall. The Delta 2 Max can recover 80% in 43 minutes — bank that energy before the grid drops again.
  • Conserve power overnight. Run only essentials while you wait for morning sun to recharge your panels.

Buyer's Guide: Sizing a Solar Generator for Storm Essentials

The most common question we get before hurricane season: "What size solar generator do I need?" The answer depends on exactly what you plan to power. Here is how to calculate your requirements.

Typical Storm Essential Loads

Refrigerator

Running: 100–200W (avg ~150W). Surge: 400–600W on compressor startup. This is your #1 priority — losing a full fridge of food costs $300–$500.

LED Lights (4–6 bulbs)

Running: 30–60W total. Minimal draw but critical for safety and sanity during multi-day outages. LED bulbs use a fraction of the power of incandescents.

Wi-Fi Router + Modem

Running: 15–25W combined. Keeps you connected to weather updates, emergency alerts, and communication with family. Worth every watt.

Phone Chargers (2–4 devices)

Running: 10–40W total. Modern phones charge efficiently. A single charge uses roughly 15–20Wh per phone.

CPAP Machine

Running: 30–60W (without heated humidifier), up to 100W with humidifier. If anyone in your household uses a CPAP, power backup is a medical necessity, not a convenience.

Window AC Unit

Running: 500–1,200W depending on BTU rating. Surge: 1,500–2,500W. This is the biggest single draw. Powering a 5,000 BTU unit in one room keeps conditions safe during heat events.

Box Fan / Floor Fan

Running: 30–75W. A low-draw alternative to AC. Three fans across the house use less power than one small AC unit.

Chest Freezer

Running: 50–100W. Surge: 200–400W. If you have stocked up on frozen food pre-storm, keeping the freezer alive prevents hundreds of dollars in waste.

Sample Power Budgets

Essential-only setup (fridge + lights + router + phones + fan): ~265W continuous draw. A 3,000Wh battery lasts ~10–11 hours. With 400W of solar panels providing daytime recharging, this setup runs indefinitely.

Comfort setup (essentials + window AC + CPAP): ~1,000–1,500W continuous draw. A 3,000Wh battery lasts ~2–3 hours. You will need to cycle the AC on and off, or expand your capacity to 6,000Wh+ for extended coverage. Solar panels are essential here — aim for 800W+ of panels.

Full household setup (essentials + AC + multiple rooms + chest freezer): ~1,500–2,000W continuous. This is where expandable systems like the AC300 (12,288Wh max) or Delta Pro (25kWh max) earn their premium. Budget for 1,000W+ of solar panels.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long will a solar generator last during a hurricane power outage?

Runtime depends on your battery capacity and total wattage draw. A 3,000Wh unit powering storm essentials — fridge (150W), router (15W), LED lights (30W), phone chargers (20W), and a fan (50W) — roughly 265W total — will last about 10–11 hours on a single charge. A larger 3,600Wh unit under the same load stretches to about 12–13 hours. The key to riding out a multi-day hurricane outage is solar recharging: with 400W of panels and decent sun between storm bands, you can recover 2,000–2,400Wh per day, making indefinite runtime realistic for essential loads. Our solar calculator can run these numbers for your specific setup.

Can I use a solar generator indoors during a hurricane?

Yes — this is one of the biggest advantages solar generators have over gas generators during hurricanes. Solar generators produce zero emissions, so they are completely safe to operate inside your home, garage, or storm shelter. Gas generators produce carbon monoxide and must be placed outdoors at least 20 feet from any window or door — which is extremely dangerous during a hurricane with high winds and flooding. Every year, carbon monoxide poisoning from improperly placed gas generators kills dozens of people during and after hurricanes. A solar generator eliminates that risk entirely. For a full comparison, see our guide on solar generators vs. gas generators.

Should I charge my solar generator before a hurricane hits?

Absolutely — charge it to 100% as soon as a hurricane watch or warning is issued for your area. Do not wait until the storm is imminent. Most solar generators charge from a wall outlet in 1.5–3 hours, so this is a quick task. The EcoFlow Delta 2 Max is the fastest at 0–80% in 43 minutes. Also charge any expansion batteries, portable battery banks, laptops, and rechargeable flashlights. If you have solar panels, test them in advance to confirm they are working, but do NOT deploy them outside during the storm — set them up after the storm passes for recharging. Pre-storm preparation is the single most important factor in how well a solar generator performs during a hurricane.

What size solar generator do I need for hurricane preparedness?

For a typical household running storm essentials — refrigerator, lights, router, phone chargers, and a fan — you need a minimum of 2,000Wh for overnight coverage (8–10 hours). For 24-hour coverage without recharging, aim for 3,000–3,600Wh. If you also need to power a window AC unit, CPAP machine, or medical equipment, go for 3,000Wh minimum with 3,000W+ output to handle the surge loads. For multi-day outages common after major hurricanes, an expandable system (BLUETTI AC300 or EcoFlow Delta Pro) with solar panels is ideal because you can recharge during breaks in the weather and extend your runtime indefinitely.
